# Research brief 01 — The Contendings of Horus and Seth

**For a researcher with web access. Everything below is pasteable as-is.**

This is the first of six retrieval tasks. It is the highest-value one, because
the timeline record it feeds was written entirely from secondary summaries and
says so in its own text. Replacing it with the manuscript is the difference
between the dataset asserting something and the dataset showing something.

---

## The standard, before anything else

This applies to every line you send back. It is not politeness — the dataset it
feeds exists specifically to expose the gap between what a source says and what
people repeat about it, so a confident paraphrase does more damage here than a
gap does.

- **Quote verbatim, with page or line references.** Do not paraphrase and
  present it as the translation.
- **Never reconstruct a transliteration from memory.** Quote the published one
  or write `NOT FOUND`.
- **If you cannot open a source, write `NOT FOUND`** rather than answering from
  general knowledge.
- **Give the URL you actually opened** for every claim, not a URL where the
  claim could plausibly be found.
- **Where the papyrus is damaged, lost or Gardiner hedges, say so.** Lacunae are
  wanted, not smoothed over. Gardiner's own footnotes flagging uncertainty are
  among the most valuable things you can send.
- **Mark your own inferences as inferences.** If you are reading a line number
  off a plate rather than off Gardiner's text, say that.

A short `NOT FOUND` list at the end is a successful result, not a failed one.

## What this is for, so you can judge what matters

The timeline record must be able to answer "why do we say that?" all the way
down to the papyrus. The chain is:

```
a statement we make  →  the episode  →  papyrus page and line
                     →  Gardiner's transcription  →  translation  →  our summary
```

Two framing points that decide what is worth extracting:

1. **The central dispute is succession to the office of Osiris.** The text is a
   legal proceeding before a divine tribunal, not a morality tale. Anything that
   shows it working as a lawsuit — arguments, precedents, procedural delay,
   appeals, letters — is high value.
2. **Seth has a case.** The most useful single thing you can send is the actual
   basis Seth gives for his claim, in Gardiner's English, with its line
   reference. Popular retellings omit it entirely, which is how a legal dispute
   becomes good-versus-evil.

Do not soften the text. Two episodes are routinely censored or euphemised in
retellings — Hathor before Re-Harakhty, and the sexual encounter between Seth
and Horus. Both are wanted, in plain academic language, because a dataset that
quietly drops the uncomfortable parts is editing its source.

## The episodes

Read Gardiner's text and extract the narrative **as it actually divides**. The
list below is what the dataset expects to find; it is a checklist, **not a
substitute for reading**. If the manuscript divides differently, follow the
manuscript and say so — that is a finding in itself. If an episode on this list
turns out not to be there, say that too.

1. **Horus claims the office of Osiris.** Horus presented as child and heir.
   What exactly is the claim of legitimate succession?
2. **Seth's counterclaim.** *Priority task.* The actual basis he gives. Verbatim,
   with line reference.
3. **The divine court.** Who participates — Re-Harakhty / the Master of the
   Universe, Thoth, the Ennead, Neith, Isis, others. How the proceeding is
   conducted, and how long it runs (the eighty years).
4. **Neith's judgment.** The message attributed to her, what she proposes, and
   how the Ennead reacts.
5. **Re-Harakhty's reaction.** The disagreement inside the court. The text does
   not show every deity instantly agreeing that Horus should rule, and that
   disagreement is the point.
6. **Hathor and Re-Harakhty.** Re-Harakhty withdraws in anger; Hathor approaches
   him; the manuscript describes her exposing herself, and his laughing and
   recovering. Record it as an ancient textual episode. A photograph of recto
   page 4 covering this section is in *Met Museum Journal* 50 (2015) — note the
   figure number and page if you can reach it.
7. **Isis intervenes.** What she actually does, not "helps Horus".
8. **Isis and Seth.** Isis in disguise obtains a statement from Seth. What she
   says, what he says, and **why his answer matters to the succession claim** —
   this is where the narrative frames his legal position, so quote it in full.
9. **The hippopotamus contest.** Terms, proposed duration, Isis's intervention,
   what happens to each of them.
10. **Horus and Isis.** The violent episode, exactly as the manuscript has it.
    Keep what the papyrus says separate from what later interpreters make of it.
11. **Seth's sexual challenge.** Seth's action, Horus's response, Isis's
    response, what becomes of each god's semen, the lettuce, and the hearing
    that follows. Neutral academic language throughout.
12. **The semen test.** Thoth's determination of where each god's semen is, and
    what the court concludes from it.
13. **The boat contest.** What Seth builds, what Horus builds, how Horus
    disguises his, what happens, how it ends.
14. **Osiris intervenes.** *Priority task.* The correspondence with the court and
    Osiris's own argument. This is the strongest evidence in the text for how it
    conceives legitimate kingship and the authority of the dead ruler — quote it
    at length.
15. **The final judgment.** *Priority task.* Who receives the office, what
    happens to Seth, **what role Seth is subsequently given**, and how the gods
    react. Not "good defeats evil" — the actual settlement, verbatim.

## Three specific questions to answer explicitly

Answer these separately at the end, each with a line reference, because the
dataset makes claims that depend on them:

1. **Is the tone of the narrative comic?** The record currently says the text is
   "closer to farce" than to solemn scripture, on secondhand authority. Quote
   two or three passages that support or undercut that, and say which way you
   think the evidence points.
2. **Is Seth ever described in terms that would justify calling him evil,** as
   opposed to violent, crude or dangerous? Quote the strongest example either
   way.
3. **What does the settlement give Seth?** If he is given a role rather than
   simply defeated, quote it. This bears directly on a separate record about
   Set's later demonisation, and on whether the demonisation is present here at
   all.

---

## What not to do

- Do not design timeline records, choose classifications, or decide what is a
  precursor versus a parallel. That needs the codebase open.
- Do not reproduce Lichtheim's translation in bulk. Short quotations for
  comparison only.
- Do not fill a line reference by inference from a summary. `NOT FOUND` is
  better than a plausible number, and a wrong line reference is worse than none
  because it looks checkable.
